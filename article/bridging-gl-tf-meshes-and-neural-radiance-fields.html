Bridging glTF Meshes and Neural Radiance Fields

The evolution of real-time 3D graphics is driving a convergence between explicit polygonal geometry and implicit neural representations like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian Splatting. As the universally adopted "JPEG of 3D," the glTF format is uniquely positioned to standardize how these disparate paradigms interact. Future revisions of the specification will likely bridge this gap by introducing standardized volumetric data structures, hybrid rendering scene graphs, and unified material pipelines that allow traditional rasterizers and neural rendering engines to coexist within a single asset.

The Fundamental Divide: Polygons vs. Neural Fields

Conventional glTF assets rely on explicit geometry: vertices, triangular faces, UV coordinates, and Physically Based Rendering (PBR) texture maps. This format excels in real-time rasterization, skeletal animation, dynamic lighting, and collision physics.

In contrast, Neural Radiance Fields and related radiance techniques model scenes volumetrically or through dense spatial approximations. Instead of defined surfaces, they store spatial coordinates, view-dependent color emissions, and density values (often encoded in neural network weights, voxel grids, or millions of oriented 3D Gaussians). Because neural assets lack explicit topological boundaries, traditional game engines and 3D applications cannot easily apply standard collision, physics, or rigging pipelines to them.

Standardizing Neural Payloads Through glTF Extensions

The most immediate path toward integration is the development of vendor-neutral glTF extensions (such as an eventual KHR_neural_field or KHR_gaussian_splatting). Rather than overhauling the core specification, the Khronos Group can leverage glTF’s extensible binary container format (.glb).

Future extensions can standardize the storage of:

  • Point and Gaussian Buffers: Packing position, covariance matrices, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients directly into binary buffer views.
  • Compressed Neural Weights: Embedding quantized weights for lightweight Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) or tri-plane representations directly within glTF's chunk structure, utilizing Draco or Meshopt-style compression tuned for high-dimensional arrays.
  • Density and Feature Grids: Encoding 3D volumetric textures and latent vectors alongside standard 2D image buffers.

Hybrid Scene Graph Architecture

A future glTF standard will likely treat neural representations as first-class scene graph nodes that can link directly to polygonal structures. This enables hybrid composition:

  1. Proxy Geometry for Interaction: A model can define a low-poly proxy mesh for raycasting, physics calculations, and broad-phase occlusion, while pointing to an embedded neural field for primary visual rendering.
  2. Deformable Neural Ensembles: By associating neural primitives with skeletal nodes in glTF’s existing skinning and joint hierarchy, future revisions will allow neural fields to be articulated, solving one of the major hurdles in animating captured neural assets.
  3. Graceful Fallbacks: Runtimes lacking neural rendering capabilities can parse the standard polygonal mesh node, while advanced renderers can skip the fallback mesh and evaluate the neural radiance payload.

Unifying Lighting with PBR Standards

Standard glTF assets achieve cross-platform visual fidelity through well-defined PBR material models. Pure NeRF captures typically bake lighting into view-dependent color emissions, which prevents an asset from interacting realistically with surrounding dynamic lights.

Future extensions will increasingly bridge this by standardizing neural relighting models. By separating neural outputs into normals, roughness, ambient occlusion, and albedo rather than raw RGB color, glTF can ensure that neural objects respect the scene’s environmental light sources, shadows, and image-based lighting (IBL) consistent with existing PBR workflows.

Progressive Streaming and Adaptive Level of Detail

Rendering complex neural radiance fields requires significant memory bandwidth and compute power. Future glTF streaming workflows will incorporate hierarchical level of detail (LoD) for neural fields. Much like mipmaps for textures or progressive LODs for polygonal meshes, glTF can organize neural data hierarchically. A renderer could stream coarse low-frequency radiance approximations or downscaled Gaussian counts first, progressively resolving high-frequency spatial details as the camera approaches.
